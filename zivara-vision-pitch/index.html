1. Executive Summary

The original idea is to create a gallery-like application for premium imitation / “master-copy” jewellery by aggregating inventory from local jewellery shops, initially starting with one partner store and then onboarding shops one by one.

Core thesis The opportunity is not simply to build a jewellery rental app. The stronger opportunity is to build the discovery and commerce layer for fragmented local jewellery inventory: discover → compare → reserve → rent or buy → eventually style.

The proposed launch model is deliberately asset-light. Jewellery stores retain ownership of inventory and fulfillment. Zivara creates digital catalogues, demand, discovery, leads and eventually transactions. This avoids the capital burden of buying inventory and allows the business to validate demand before scaling.

2. The Business Thesis

Zivara should be thought of as “Pinterest / Instagram-style discovery + local jewellery marketplace + rental infrastructure,” rather than as another jewellery store.

“Discover jewellery near you. Rent it for the moment. Buy it if you love it.”

The long-term company

Consumer layer: a highly visual jewellery discovery experience.

Commerce layer: rental, purchase, reservation and eventually delivery.

Supply layer: a network of local stores whose fragmented inventory becomes searchable and discoverable.

Software layer: eventually provide participating stores with catalogue, inventory, leads, booking and analytics tools.

Local shops→Zivara catalogue→Discovery→Enquiry→Rent / Buy→More shops

3. Market & Product-Market-Fit Hypothesis

There is evidence for the underlying behavior, but the exact Zivara model still needs direct customer and retailer validation.

Online jewellery tailwind

A SEBI-hosted industry document citing CareEdge Research estimated India's online jewellery market at about ₹262 billion in CY24 and projected ₹743 billion by CY29, a 23.2% CAGR from CY24–CY29. The same source described online penetration as only around 4–5% at the time, indicating room for digital discovery and commerce. Source.

Indian jewellery demand remains substantial

The World Gold Council reported that India accounted for 22% of global jewellery demand in Q1 2026 and noted continued momentum in digital channels, while higher prices pushed mass-market consumers toward lighter-weight, lower-carat and studded jewellery. Source.

Chennai already validates rental behavior

Local businesses advertise bridal jewellery rental, online browsing, WhatsApp enquiry/booking, styling and delivery. This is strong evidence that customers already understand the rental proposition. It also means Zivara must differentiate through aggregation, discovery and convenience rather than merely offering rental. Jazz Collections · Fine Shine.

4. The Problem

Customer pain

  • Jewellery discovery is fragmented across Instagram, WhatsApp and physical stores.
  • Customers often have to contact several sellers to compare styles and prices.
  • Rental availability and pricing can be difficult to understand quickly.
  • Finding a complete matching look is harder than finding a single product.
  • Customers may not know which stores are nearby or trustworthy.

Retailer pain

  • Large collections are difficult to expose digitally.
  • Instagram and WhatsApp are useful but weak as inventory systems.
  • Owners spend time repeatedly sending photos and prices.
  • Local stores struggle to reach customers beyond their immediate network.
  • There is limited structured data about what customers browse and request.
The wedge: digitize inventory once, make it discoverable repeatedly, and send qualified demand back to the store.

6. Business Model

Start with the simplest revenue model that aligns Zivara with retailer success. Add SaaS and other revenue streams only after transaction volume exists.

Revenue streamHow it worksWhen
Transaction commissionTake a percentage of rental or purchase transactions generated by Zivara.Start here
Qualified lead feeRetailer pays for a verified customer enquiry.Early experiment
Retailer SaaSMonthly fee for digital catalogue, inventory, leads and analytics.After product-market fit
Featured placementSponsored discovery / promoted collections.After traffic
Logistics feeDelivery / pickup / fulfillment margin.Later
Recommended starting principle: “You don't pay us unless we generate business.” This reduces retailer resistance and gives Zivara a strong pilot proposition.

Illustrative economics — not a forecast

Example only: a ₹4,000 rental at a 15% marketplace commission would generate ₹600 gross revenue to Zivara. A ₹10,000 purchase at 10% would generate ₹1,000. Actual take rates must be negotiated and validated by retailer interviews, margins, GST/tax treatment, refunds, payment costs and operational responsibilities.

7. Go-to-Market Strategy

Supply-first launch

  1. Identify 10 promising jewellery shops.
  2. Interview owners about inventory, rentals, purchases, Instagram, WhatsApp and customer acquisition.
  3. Select one responsive pilot partner.
  4. Digitize the best 100–200 pieces rather than attempting the entire inventory.
  5. Launch the catalogue and manually route enquiries to the partner.
  6. Measure transactions before aggressively onboarding more stores.

Consumer acquisition

Instagram / Reels“5 jewellery sets under ₹5K rental”, before/after looks, saree matching and bridal styling.
Wedding ecosystemBridal boutiques, makeup artists, photographers, wedding planners, mehendi artists and venues.
Referral loopsCustomer referrals + retailer referrals + stylist referrals.
Local SEOPages for “bridal jewellery rental in [area]”, “muhurtham jewellery”, etc.
WhatsAppUse it as a conversion channel initially rather than fighting existing customer habits.
Micro-influencersChennai bridal/fashion creators with strong local trust rather than only large influencers.

Geographic strategy

Do not start with “all Chennai.” Start with a manageable cluster or category, prove density, then expand: micro-market → Chennai → Tamil Nadu → South India → India.

8. MVP Specification

Customer MVP

  • Beautiful visual homepage
  • Category / occasion browsing
  • Search + filters
  • Product detail page
  • Rental price
  • Purchase price where applicable
  • Shop information + location
  • Availability enquiry
  • WhatsApp / reserve CTA
  • Basic lead tracking

Admin MVP

  • Shop management
  • Product CRUD
  • Image management
  • Rental / sale pricing
  • Availability status
  • Lead management
  • Booking status
  • Simple analytics
Do not overbuild. The first version does not need complex authentication, microservices, automated logistics, multi-agent AI, sophisticated payments or a retailer dashboard. Manually operate the difficult parts until demand is proven.

Suggested technical foundation

Next.js + TypeScript frontend, FastAPI or Node backend if needed, PostgreSQL or SQLite for the pilot, object storage for images, WhatsApp for conversion, and a simple admin interface. Architecture should remain replaceable; business validation is more important than technical sophistication at this stage.

9. Product & Business Roadmap

Phase 0 — Discovery

Interview 20–30 consumers and 10–15 retailers. Select the pilot shop. Establish pricing and commission hypotheses.

Phase 1 — Catalogue

One shop, 100–200 curated products, visual gallery, WhatsApp enquiries and manual fulfilment.

Phase 2 — Marketplace

5–20 shops, structured inventory, location discovery, comparison, reservations and transaction tracking.

Phase 3 — Commerce

Online payment, availability calendar, rental workflow, deposits, delivery/pickup integrations and customer accounts.

Phase 4 — AI Discovery

Visual search, outfit matching, AI stylist, personalized recommendations and complete-look generation.

Phase 5 — Retailer Infrastructure

Inventory management, CRM, analytics, customer insights, demand forecasting and a retailer SaaS product.

14. Founder Operating Plan

The founder's job in the first months is not to build the perfect app. It is to discover whether a repeatable local marketplace transaction can be created.

First 30 days

  1. Talk to 10–15 jewellery retailers.
  2. Talk to 20–30 potential customers.
  3. Map current discovery, rental and purchase workflows.
  4. Select one pilot retailer.
  5. Digitize 100–200 products.
  6. Launch a polished web catalogue.
  7. Drive the first 100–300 visitors manually.
  8. Track every enquiry and transaction.

Questions every retailer should answer

  • How many jewellery enquiries arrive through Instagram and WhatsApp?
  • What proportion of business is rental vs purchase?
  • What are typical rental prices and durations?
  • How is availability tracked?
  • What are the most requested categories?
  • What happens when a customer wants a design they do not stock?
  • Would they share catalogue images and pricing?
  • Would they pay commission for a transaction generated by Zivara?
  • What would make them stop using the platform?

16. Immediate Next Steps

Do not build the full marketplace yet.
The next milestone is not “launch the app.” It is: prove that one jewellery retailer will share inventory and that Zivara can generate qualified customer demand.
  1. Brand validation: verify Zivara trademark, domain and social availability.
  2. Retailer research: interview 10–15 Chennai shops.
  3. Customer research: interview 20–30 target customers.
  4. Pilot: secure one shop and 100–200 products.
  5. MVP: launch visual catalogue + enquiry/WhatsApp workflow.
  6. Demand test: use Instagram, wedding partners and local SEO.
  7. Measure: views → enquiries → reservations → transactions.
  8. Decision: only after evidence, expand to 5–20 shops.

Long-term vision: become the discovery, commerce and software layer connecting India's fragmented jewellery retail ecosystem with modern consumers.
